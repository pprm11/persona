# Cost to trusted outcome and cost of the machinery

Evaluate the work the beneficiary needs, not output volume. More generated code, more agents or
a shorter time-to-diff can coexist with slower review, rework and unreliable adoption.

Separate these observations:

- Automated elapsed/critical path, queue/setup time and resource consumption.
- Human review/decision/reconstruction effort, only when actually observed or clearly estimated.
- Repair, repeat attempts, regressions, escapes and legitimate cases rejected.
- Recurring maintenance of prompts, rules, skills, context, schemas, plugins and integrations.
- Consumer adoption, version migration, support, distribution and storage/operation.

Do not infer human attention from a timer or cost from tokens without price/categorization.
Preserve unknowns, failed/cancelled attempts and unit/cohort/denominator. Do not add parallel wall
times or count the same request once per enclosing scope. Before/after comparisons require a
comparable result and quality boundary; statistics from another team/tool are not local ROI.

For a shared framework, one useful hypothesis is:

`avoided consumer duplication and repair > shared maintenance + per-consumer migration + operation`

Identify the unknown terms rather than filling them with marketing assumptions. Two consumers
can justify an experiment; they do not establish fleet-wide savings. If a new layer costs more
than the manual work it removes, simplify or stop it while preserving the required outcome.

Consider build versus maintained service only with the actual non-core pain: compatibility,
security, data handling, vendor dependency, migration/exit cost and total maintenance. A vendor
can remove part of the machinery while leaving product authority and acceptance with the team.
Neither an internal platform nor buying one is the automatic next step.

Set an experiment boundary and revisit condition. A measured no-go, smaller mechanism or decision
to defer an optional judge/pack/parallel executor is a legitimate outcome. Do not respond to a
failed value hypothesis by generating a larger factory.
